

Contrastive Analysis: Category 1 (Tense & Form)
Target: 10-12 Entries Delivered: 10 Entries
Item ID: tense-2-A
Original Question: "Please be quiet! The children ___." (Options: sleep, sleeps, are sleeping)
Correct Answer: are sleeping

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"S'il vous plaît, soyez silencieux ! Les enfants dorment."
(French uses the Présent Simple: "dorment" to describe an action happening right now.)

English Structure:
"Please be quiet! The children are sleeping."
(English *must* use the Present Continuous: "are + -ing" for an action in progress at this exact moment.)

Why This Is Hard:
French uses the same simple present tense for both habits ("Ils dorment la nuit") and actions happening now ("Ils dorment"). English speakers *must* choose. French speakers often use the Present Simple ("The children sleep") because it's a direct translation of "Les enfants dorment."

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "The children sleep."
- GREEN (Correct English): "The children are sleeping."
Item ID: tense-3-A
Original Question: "I ___ this exercise is not very difficult." (Options: am thinking, think, thinks)
Correct Answer: think

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Je pense que cet exercice n'est pas très difficile."
(French uses the simple present "Je pense" for an opinion.)

English Structure:
"I think this exercise is not very difficult."
(English also uses the Present Simple for opinions, beliefs, and "stative" verbs. We do not use the continuous form.)

Why This Is Hard:
After learning the "be + -ing" rule (from the item above), students often *over-use* it. They apply it to all verbs, including "stative" (state) verbs like 'think', 'know', 'like', and 'want'. In this case, the French "Je pense" is a good clue to use the simple "I think."

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "I am thinking this exercise..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "I think this exercise..."
Item ID: tense-6-A
Original Question: "Yesterday, I ___ my uncle at the motor park." (Options: see, saw, have seen)
Correct Answer: saw

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Hier, j'ai vu mon oncle à la gare routière."
(French uses the *Passé Composé* ("j'ai vu") for almost all completed past actions.)

English Structure:
"Yesterday, I saw my uncle at the motor park."
(English *must* use the Past Simple ("saw") because the time "Yesterday" is specific and finished.)

Why This Is Hard:
The French *Passé Composé* ("j'ai vu") looks identical to the English *Present Perfect* ("I have seen"). Students translate directly and use the Present Perfect, but English *requires* the Past Simple for any action with a finished time (like yesterday, last week, in 2010).

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "Yesterday, I have seen my uncle..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "Yesterday, I saw my uncle..."
Item ID: tense-12-A
Original Question: "Mr. Foncha ___ a teacher since 1995." (Options: is, was, has been)
Correct Answer: has been

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"M. Foncha est professeur depuis 1995."
(French uses the *Présent Simple* ("est") + "depuis" to describe an action that started in the past and continues to the present.)

English Structure:
"Mr. Foncha has been a teacher since 1995."
(English *must* use the *Present Perfect* ("has been") + "since" for this situation.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is one of the most common and direct structural interferences. Translating "est... depuis" directly results in the error "He is... since". English *requires* the Present Perfect (have/has + participle) to connect the past to the present in this way.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "Mr. Foncha is a teacher since 1995."
- GREEN (Correct English): "Mr. Foncha has been a teacher since 1995."
Item ID: tense-16-A
Original Question: "We ___ our grandmother in the village next weekend. We bought the tickets." (Options: will visit, are going to visit, visit)
Correct Answer: are going to visit

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Nous allons visiter notre grand-mère... (Futur Proche)
"Nous visiterons notre grand-mère... (Futur Simple)

English Structure:
"We are going to visit our grandmother..."
(English uses "be going to" for a *plan* that was made *before* the moment of speaking. "We bought the tickets" proves it's a plan.)

Why This Is Hard:
French "futur simple" ("visiterons") is often taught as the translation for "will visit". Students use "will" for all future actions. However, English distinguishes between a plan ('be going to') and an instant decision/promise ('will').

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "We will visit our grandmother..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "We are going to visit our grandmother..."
Item ID: tense-17-A
Original Question: "It's very hot in this classroom." "You are right. I ___ open the window." (Options: am going to, will, am)
Correct Answer: will

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Il fait très chaud..." "Tu as raison. Je vais ouvrir la fenêtre."
(French often uses the *Futur Proche* ("je vais ouvrir") for an instant decision made at the moment of speaking.)

English Structure:
"I will open the window."
(English *must* use "will" for an *instant decision* or offer made at the moment of speaking.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is the reverse of the previous item. Students translate the *Futur Proche* ("je vais") directly to "I am going to", but English reserves "will" for this specific situation of a spontaneous decision.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "I am going to open the window."
- GREEN (Correct English): "I will open the window."
Item ID: tense-24-A
Original Question: "You must ___ your homework before you watch TV." (Options: to do, do, doing)
Correct Answer: do

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Tu dois faire tes devoirs..."
(French modal verbs like *devoir* (must), *pouvoir* (can), and *vouloir* (want) are followed by the *infinitive* form of the next verb: "faire", "aller", "parler".)

English Structure:
"You must do your homework..."
(English modal verbs (must, can, should, will) are *always* followed by the *base verb* (the infinitive *without* 'to').)

Why This Is Hard:
French speakers are trained to use the full infinitive after a modal. They translate this logic by adding "to" after English modals, creating the very common "must to do", "can to go", or "should to study" errors.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "You must to do your homework."
- GREEN (Correct English): "You must do your homework."
Item ID: tense-25-A
Original Question: Find the error: "My father cans drive a large truck." (Error: cans drive)
Correct Answer: can drive

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Mon père peut conduire un grand camion."
(French modal-like verbs are conjugated. The verb "pouvoir" changes from the infinitive to "peut" for the 3rd person "il".)

English Structure:
"My father can drive a large truck."
(English modal verbs (can, must, should) *never* change. They do not take an "-s" for the third person.)

Why This Is Hard:
French speakers learn to conjugate all verbs for the subject (je peux, tu peux, il peut). They apply this rule to English modals and add an "-s" for "he/she/it", resulting in "cans", "musts", or "shoulds".

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "My father cans drive..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "My father can drive..."
Item ID: tense-4-A
Original Question: "Where is Muna?" "He ___ football in the school compound." (Options: plays, is playing, play)
Correct Answer: is playing

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Où est Muna ?" "Il joue au football..."
(Again, French uses the *Présent Simple* ("joue") to describe the action happening now.)

English Structure:
"Where is Muna?" "He is playing football..."
(English *must* use the Present Continuous ("is playing") to answer a question about what someone is doing *at this moment*.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is a high-frequency example of the "Présent Simple vs. Present Continuous" error. The French "Il joue" is translated directly to "He plays", which in English means he plays habitually (e.g., "He plays for the school team"), not right now.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "He plays football..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "He is playing football..."
Item ID: tense-10-A
Original Question: Find the error: "My family has moved to Bafoussam in 2018." (Error: has moved)
Correct Answer: moved

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Ma famille a déménagé à Bafoussam en 2018."
(French uses the *Passé Composé* ("a déménagé") for this finished action.)

English Structure:
"My family moved to Bafoussam in 2018."
(English *must* use the *Past Simple* ("moved") because "in 2018" is a specific, finished time in the past.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is the same error as `tense-6-A`. The French *Passé Composé* ("a déménagé") looks like the English *Present Perfect* ("has moved"). Students use this form by default, forgetting that if a specific finished time is mentioned, the Past Simple is required.

Contrastive Analysis: Category 2 (Subject-Verb Agreement)
Target: 8-10 Entries Delivered: 8 Entries
Item ID: sv-1-A
Original Question: "My uncle ___ in Yaoundé." (Options: live, lives, is living)
Correct Answer: lives

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Mon oncle habite à Yaoundé."
(The verb "habiter" is conjugated: "j'habite", "tu habites", "il habite". The sound of "habite" (il) is the same as "habite" (je).)

English Structure:
"My uncle lives in Yaoundé."
(English adds a very clear "-s" sound to the verb for the 3rd person singular: He, She, It. "I live" vs. "He lives".)

Why This Is Hard:
In spoken French, the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd person singular forms of many verbs sound identical (e.g., "je parle", "tu parles", "il parle" all sound like "parl"). French speakers are not used to listening for or pronouncing a final 's' to show the 3rd person, so they forget to add it.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "My uncle live..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "My uncle lives..."
Item ID: sv-3-A
Original Question: "Ngono ___ like couscous." (Options: don't, not, doesn't)
Correct Answer: doesn't

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Ngono n'aime pas le couscous."
(French uses "ne...pas" around the main verb "aime" to make it negative.)

English Structure:
"Ngono doesn't like couscous."
(English *must* use the auxiliary verb "do" + "not". For a 3rd person subject (Ngono = she), "do" becomes "does".)

Why This Is Hard:
French has no "do/does" auxiliary. Students translate the "ne...pas" structure directly, resulting in "Ngono likes not" (from "n'aime pas") or they use "don't" for all subjects, forgetting to change "do" to "does" for "she".

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "Ngono like not..." or "Ngono don't like..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "Ngono doesn't like..."
Item ID: sv-5-A
Original Question: "The students ___ their uniforms to school." (Options: wears, wear, is wearing)
Correct Answer: wear

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Les étudiants portent leur uniforme..."
(The 3rd person plural verb "portent" is used. It sounds different from the singular "porte".)

English Structure:
"The students wear their uniforms..."
(The English plural subject "students" (they) uses the *base verb* "wear". There is *no* "-s".)

Why This Is Hard:
This is the *reverse* of the first error. After learning the "He/She + -s" rule, students get confused and think a plural subject (students) should have a plural verb (with -s). This is the opposite of English logic. In English: Plural Noun (students) = Singular Verb (wear).

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "The students wears..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "The students wear..."
Item ID: sv-9-A
Original Question: "___ a lot of bendskins (moto-taxis) in Douala." (Options: There is, There are, It is)
Correct Answer: There are

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Il y a beaucoup de bendskins à Douala."
(French uses "Il y a" for *both* singular ("Il y a un problème") and plural ("Il y a des problèmes"). The verb "a" never changes.)

English Structure:
"There are a lot of bendskins in Douala."
(English *must* change the verb to match the noun that follows. "There is" for singular, "There are" for plural.)

Why This Is Hard:
French speakers are used to the fixed phrase "Il y a" and translate it directly to "There is" in all situations. They forget to check if the noun ("bendskins") is plural and change the verb to "are".

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "There is a lot of bendskins..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "There are a lot of bendskins..."
Item ID: sv-13-A
Original Question: "In Cameroon, ___ love football." (Options: people, people loves, people love)
Correct Answer: people love

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Au Cameroun, les gens aiment le football."
(French "les gens" (people) is a plural noun and takes a plural verb, "aiment".)

English Structure:
"In Cameroon, people love football."
(English "people" is *always* a plural noun (it is the plural of 'person') and takes a plural verb, "love" (no -s).)

Why This Is Hard:
Although French ("les gens sont/aiment") is also plural, students often mistake the English word "people" as a singular collective noun (like "team" or "family"). They incorrectly add an "-s" to the verb ("people loves") or use a singular verb ("people is").

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "people loves..." or "people is..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "people love..." or "people are..."
Item ID: sv-14-A
Original Question: "Our football team ___ playing a match in Garoua today." (Options: is, are, be)
Correct Answer: is

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Notre équipe de football joue un match..."
(French "équipe" (team) is a singular noun and takes a singular verb, "joue".)

English Structure:
"Our football team is playing a match..."
(In English, collective nouns (team, family, government, class) are *usually* treated as a single singular unit ("it"). Therefore, they take a singular verb "is".)

Why This Is Hard:
This is a point of confusion. While French also uses a singular verb here ("l'équipe est..."), students often get confused by the *idea* of many players. They think "team = many people" and incorrectly choose the plural verb "are".

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "Our football team are playing..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "Our football team is playing..."
Item ID: sv-18-A
Original Question: "One of the students ___ left his book." (Options: have, has, -)
Correct Answer: has

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Un des étudiants a laissé son livre."
(The subject is "Un" (One) and it takes the singular verb "a" (has). "des étudiants" is a prepositional phrase.)

English Structure:
"One of the students has left his book."
(The true subject is "One" (singular). The verb must agree with "One", not with the nearby plural noun "students". Therefore, we use "has".)

Why This Is Hard:
The plural noun "students" is right next to the verb, which acts as a "distractor". Students see "students" and automatically choose the plural verb "have". This is a common error even though the French structure ("Un... a") is logically the same.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "One of the students have left..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "One of the students has left..."
Item ID: sv-19-A
Original Question: "The price of these plantains ___ too high." (Options: are, is, be)
Correct Answer: is

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Le prix de ces plantains est trop élevé."
(The subject is "Le prix" (The price), which is singular. The verb "est" (is) agrees with "prix", not with "plantains".)

English Structure:
"The price of these plantains is too high."
(This is the same logic as the item above. The subject is "price" (singular). The phrase "of these plantains" is an interrupter. The verb "is" must agree with "price".)

Why This Is Hard:
This is another "distractor" problem. The plural noun "plantains" is the closest word to the verb, so students make the verb agree with it ("plantains are..."). They must be trained to find the *true* subject ("price").

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "The price of these plantains are..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "The price of these plantains is..."



Contrastive Analysis: Category 3 (Prepositions)
Target: 8-10 Entries Delivered: 9 Entries
Item ID: prep-1-A
Original Question: "My birthday is ___ August." (Options: on, at, in)
Correct Answer: in

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Mon anniversaire est en août."
(French uses "en" for months, which is a common equivalent for "in".)

English Structure:
"My birthday is in August."
(English uses "in" for long periods like months, seasons, and years.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is not the hardest error, as "en" often maps to "in". The confusion arises when students mix this up with "on" (for days) and "at" (for times). The French "au mois d'août" (in the month of August) can also add confusion.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "My birthday is *on* August" (confusing with days)
- GREEN (Correct English): "My birthday is *in* August"
Item ID: prep-2-A
Original Question: "The school re-opens ___ September 15th." (Options: on, at, in)
Correct Answer: on

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"L'école rouvre le 15 septembre."
(French uses the definite article "le" (the) before a specific date.)

English Structure:
"The school re-opens on September 15th."
(English *must* use the preposition "on" for all specific days and dates.)

Why This Is Hard:
French does not use a preposition like "on" in this case ("le 15"). Students translating this logic either use "the" (a direct translation of "le") or no preposition at all. They must learn the English rule: **Day/Date = ON**.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "...re-opens *the* September 15th." or "...re-opens *in* September 15th."
- GREEN (Correct English): "...re-opens *on* September 15th."
Item ID: prep-5-A
Original Question: Find the error: "...but he forgets **in** night."
Correct Answer: at night

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Il oublie la nuit" or "dans la nuit".
(French can use "la nuit" (the night) or "dans la nuit" (in the night) depending on the context.)

English Structure:
"He forgets at night."
(English uses fixed expressions: "in the morning", "in the afternoon", "in the evening", but it *must* be "at night".)

Why This Is Hard:
Students learn "in the morning/afternoon" and logically apply it, saying "in the night". This is a direct translation of "dans la nuit" and seems to follow the pattern, but "at night" is a fixed, idiomatic exception.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "He forgets *in the night*."
- GREEN (Correct English): "He forgets *at night*."
Item ID: prep-6-A
Original Question: "I live ___ a small neighborhood ___ Yaoundé." (Options: at / in, in / at, in / in)
Correct Answer: in / in

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"J'habite dans un petit quartier à Yaoundé."
(French uses "dans" (in) for an enclosed area, but "à" for a city.)

English Structure:
"I live in a small neighborhood in Yaoundé."
(English uses "in" for both the enclosed area (neighborhood) and the large area (city, country).)

Why This Is Hard:
This is a major, high-frequency error. French *always* uses "à" before a city name ("à Yaoundé", "à Douala"). Students translate "à" directly to "at". They *must* learn the English rule: **Cities & Countries = IN**.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "I live *at* Yaoundé."
- GREEN (Correct English): "I live *in* Yaoundé."
Item ID: prep-10-A
Original Question: "We are travelling ___ Bamenda tomorrow." (Options: to, at, in)
Correct Answer: to

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Nous voyageons à Bamenda demain."
(French uses "à" to show direction/destination.)

(English *must* use "to" to show movement or direction towards a destination.)

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "We are travelling *at* Bamenda."
- GREEN (Correct English): "We are travelling *to* Bamenda."
Item ID: prep-12-A
Original Question: "My father arrived ___ the village late last night." (Options: to, at, in)
Correct Answer: at

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Mon père est arrivé au village (à + le) tard hier soir."
(French uses "à" (in its 'au' form) for "arriver".)

English Structure:
"My father arrived at the village..."

Why This Is Hard:
Because "go to", "travel to", and "come to" are correct, students assume "arrive to" is also correct. This is reinforced by the French "arriver à". They must learn that "arrive" is a special verb that takes "at" or "in".

Contrastive Analysis: Category 4 (Word Order)
Target: 6-8 Entries Delivered: 6 Entries
Item ID: word-1-A
Original Question: "I am looking for my ___." (Options: shoes black, black shoes, shoes blacks)
Correct Answer: black shoes

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Je cherche mes chaussures noires."
(In French, descriptive adjectives, especially for color, almost always come *after* the noun.)

English Structure:
"I am looking for my black shoes."
(In English, descriptive adjectives *almost always* come *before* the noun.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is a direct, structural habit. French speakers are used to the "Noun + Adjective" pattern ("chaussures noires") and translate it directly, resulting in the classic "shoes black" error.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "I am looking for my *shoes black*."
- GREEN (Correct English): "I am looking for my *black shoes*."
Item ID: word-4-A
Original Question: "My mother ___ goes to the market on Saturdays." (Options: always, goes always, is always)
Correct Answer: always

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Ma mère va toujours au marché le samedi."
(In French, adverbs of frequency like "toujours" (always) or "souvent" (often) typically come *after* the verb they modify: "va toujours".)

English Structure:
"My mother always goes to the market on Saturdays."
(In English, these adverbs *almost always* come *before* the main verb: "always goes".)

Why This Is Hard:
This is another direct translation of sentence structure. The French "verb + adverb" order is applied to English, creating the "goes always" error.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "My mother *goes always* to the market..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "My mother *always goes* to the market..."
Item ID: word-5-A
Original Question: "The students ___ late for class." (Options: are never, never are, be never)
Correct Answer: are never

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Les étudiants ne sont jamais en retard..."
(In French, "ne...jamais" (never) is placed *around* the verb "sont" (are).)

English Structure:
"The students are never late for class."
(This is the main exception to the previous rule: adverbs of frequency come *after* the verb "to be" (am, is, are, was, were).)

Why This Is Hard:
The English rules for adverbs are confusing. Students learn "adverb *before* the verb" (from Q4) and apply it here, saying "never are". The "ne...jamais" structure in French doesn't help, as it's completely different.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "The students *never are* late..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "The students *are never* late..."
Item ID: word-7-A
Original Question: "___ your homework?" (Options: You finished, Did you finish, You did finish)
Correct Answer: Did you finish

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Tu as fini tes devoirs ?" or "Est-ce que tu as fini... ?"
(Spoken French very commonly just raises the intonation of a statement: "Tu as fini ?". Or it uses the fixed phrase "Est-ce que".)

English Structure:
"Did you finish your homework?"
(English *must* use an auxiliary verb ("Did" for past simple) and *invert* it with the subject: "Did you...".)

Why This Is Hard:
The "statement with rising intonation" (Tu as fini?) is the most common way to ask a question in spoken French. Students translate this directly, saying "You finished?" (like Q10, "You like?"). This is a very deep habit that must be broken.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "You finished your homework?"
- GREEN (Correct English): "Did you finish your homework?"
Item ID: word-8-A
Original Question: "Where ___ Ngono live?" (Options: does, is, -)
Correct Answer: does

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Où est-ce que Ngono habite ?" or "Où Ngono habite-t-elle ?"
(French either uses "Est-ce que" (which requires no inversion) or inverts the main verb and subject: "habite-t-elle".)

English Structure:
"Where does Ngono live?"
(English *must* use the auxiliary "do/does" for a simple present question. The structure is: **Wh-Word + Auxiliary (Does) + Subject (Ngono) + Verb (live)**.)

Why This Is Hard:
French *never* uses "do/does". Students forget this auxiliary completely and try to invert the main verb ("Where lives Ngono?"), which is archaic, or they don't invert at all ("Where Ngono lives?"), which is a direct translation of the "Où est-ce que..." structure.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "Where Ngono lives?" or "Where lives Ngono?"
- GREEN (Correct English): "Where does Ngono live?"
Item ID: word-11-A
Original Question: "I ___ like to eat koki." (Options: not, no, do not)
Correct Answer: do not

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Je n'aime pas manger le koki."
(French creates a negative by putting "ne...pas" *around* the main verb: "n'aime pas".)

English Structure:
"I do not like to eat koki."
(English *must* use the auxiliary verb "do" + "not" *before* the main verb.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is a core structural difference. Because French places "pas" (not) *after* the verb, students translate this directly and say, "I like not...". They must learn that English *requires* the "do" helper verb to form a negative.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "I *like not*..." or "I *no like*..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "I *do not like*..."

Item ID: plurality-2-A
Original Question: "How many ___ do you have in your family?" (Options: brother, brothers, brotheres)
Correct Answer: brothers

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Combien de frères as-tu dans ta famille ?"
(The plural 's' on "frères" is silent. "frère" and "frères" are pronounced exactly the same.)

English Structure:
"How many brothers do you have in your family?"
(The plural 's' on "brothers" is *required* and must be pronounced.)

Why This Is Hard:
Because the plural 's' is silent in spoken French, students are not used to hearing or pronouncing it. They often forget to add the 's' in English (both in writing and speaking) when a noun is plural.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "How many *brother*..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "How many *brothers*..."
Item ID: plurality-4-A
Original Question: "My uncle has four ___." (Options: child, childs, children)
Correct Answer: children

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Mon oncle a quatre enfants."
(The plural of "enfant" (child) is "enfants", which is a regular plural, even if the 's' is silent.)

English Structure:
"My uncle has four children."
(The plural of "child" is irregular. It does not take '-s'. The plural is "children".)

Why This Is Hard:
Students learn the English rule "add -s for plural" and apply it to all nouns. This creates the common error "childs". They must memorize that "children" is the correct, irregular plural form.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "four *childs*."
- GREEN (Correct English): "four *children*."
Item ID: plurality-5-A
Original Question: Find the error: "Many people in Cameroon is bilingual." (Error: is bilingual)
Correct Answer: are bilingual

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Beaucoup de gens au Cameroun sont bilingues."
(The French word "les gens" (people) is plural and correctly takes the plural verb "sont" (are).)

English Structure:
"Many people in Cameroon are bilingual."
("People" is the irregular plural of "person". It is *always* plural and *must* take a plural verb like "are".)

Why This Is Hard:
Even though French uses a plural verb here, students often get confused in English. They see "people" as a single group (like "family" or "team") and incorrectly use the singular verb "is".

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "Many people *is*..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "Many people *are*..."
Item ID: plurality-6-A
Original Question: "The teacher gave us some ___ about the test." (Options: information, informations, an information)
Correct Answer: information

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Le professeur nous a donné des informations sur le test."
(French treats "information" as a countable noun. It can be plural: "une information" (one piece) or "des informations" (some information).)

English Structure:
"The teacher gave us some information about the test."
(English "information" is *uncountable*. It *never* takes an '-s' and cannot be used with 'a/an'.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is a major structural difference. Students directly translate the French plural "des informations" to the English error "informations". They must learn that "information" is *always* singular in English.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "gave us some *informations*."
- GREEN (Correct English): "gave us some *information*."
Item ID: plurality-7-A
Original Question: "...you should ask your father for ___." (Options: an advice, advices, advice)
Correct Answer: advice

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Tu devrais demander des conseils à ton père."
(French "un conseil" is countable. The plural is "des conseils" (some advice / pieces of advice).)

English Structure:
"...you should ask your father for advice."
(Like 'information', English "advice" is *uncountable*. It *never* takes an '-s' and cannot be used with 'a/an'.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is the same error as 'information'. The French plural "des conseils" is directly translated to "advices". Students must memorize that "advice" is always singular. To count it, they must say "a *piece of* advice".

- RED (French interference): "ask for *advices*." or "ask for *an advice*."
- GREEN (Correct English): "ask for *advice*."



Contrastive Analysis: Category 6 (Articles)
Target: 4-6 Entries Delivered: 5 Entries
Item ID: articles-1-A
Original Question: "In general, ___ plantains are expensive in the dry season." (Options: the, a, Ø)
Correct Answer: Ø (no article)

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"En général, les plantains coûtent cher..."
(French *must* use the definite article "les" (the) when making a general statement about a plural noun.)

English Structure:
"In general, plantains are expensive..."
(English *must* use the "zero article" (no article) when making a general statement about a plural noun.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is a core, opposite rule. French speakers are trained to use "les" for general plurals ("J'aime les chiens"), so they translate this directly and say "I like the dogs" or "The plantains are expensive".

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "In general, *the plantains* are expensive..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "In general, *plantains* are expensive..."
Item ID: articles-2-A
Original Question: "___ life in Yaoundé is very fast." (Options: The, A, Ø)
Correct Answer: Ø (no article)

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"La vie à Yaoundé est très rapide."
(French *must* use the definite article "la" (the) when making a general statement about an abstract noun like 'life'.)

English Structure:
"Life in Yaoundé is very fast."
(English *must* use the "zero article" (no article) for abstract nouns used in a general sense.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is the same error as the one above, but for abstract nouns. French "La vie est belle" or "L'éducation est importante" is translated directly to "The life is beautiful" or "The education is important", which is incorrect in English.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "*The life* in Yaoundé..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "*Life* in Yaoundé..."
Item ID: articles-3-A
Original Question: "My aunt is ___ nurse." (Options: Ø, a, an)
Correct Answer: a

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Ma tante est infirmière."
(French does *not* use an article ("un" or "une") after the verb "être" (to be) when stating a profession.)

English Structure:
"My aunt is a nurse."
(English *requires* the article "a" or "an" before a singular, countable noun like a profession.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is another opposite rule. Students directly translate the French "est infirmière" ("is nurse") and omit the article "a".

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "My aunt is *nurse*."
- GREEN (Correct English): "My aunt is *a nurse*."
Item ID: articles-6-A
Original Question: "Can you give me ___ advice?" (Options: an, some, the)
Correct Answer: some

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Peux-tu me donner un conseil ?"
(French treats "un conseil" (a piece of advice) as a countable noun. It has a plural form, "des conseils".)

English Structure:
"Can you give me some advice?"
(English treats "advice" as *uncountable*. It cannot be plural (no "advices") and cannot be used with "a" or "an".)

Why This Is Hard:
Students directly translate the countable "un conseil" to "an advice". This error also applies to "information" ("une information" -> "an information") and "luggage" ("un bagage" -> "a luggage").

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "Can you give me *an advice*?" or "...*advices*."
- GREEN (Correct English): "Can you give me *some advice*?" or "...*a piece of advice*."
Item ID: articles-9-A
Original Question: "Muna is sick. He is in ___ hospital." (Options: Ø, the, a)
Correct Answer: Ø (no article)

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Muna est malade. Il est à l'hôpital." (à + le)
(French uses the definite article "l'hôpital" (the hospital) when talking about being at this institution.)

English Structure:
"Muna is sick. He is in hospital."
(English uses the "zero article" (no article) for institutions like 'hospital', 'school', 'church', and 'prison' when referring to their *purpose* (being a patient, student, etc.) rather than the specific building.)

Why This Is Hard:
Students translate the French "à l'hôpital" ("at the hospital") or "à l'école" ("at the school") directly and include "the". (Saying "He is in *the* hospital" is not wrong, but it means he is *physically inside the building*, perhaps just visiting. "In hospital" means he is a patient.)

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "He is in *the hospital*."
- GREEN (Correct English): "He is *in hospital*."



Contrastive Analysis: Category 7 (Auxiliaries)
Target: 4-6 Entries Delivered: 5 Entries
Item ID: aux-1-A
Original Question: "My father ___ work in Douala; he works in Yaoundé." (Options: not, don't, doesn't)
Correct Answer: doesn't

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Mon père ne travaille pas à Douala..."
(French forms the negative by placing "ne...pas" *around* the main verb "travaille".)

English Structure:
"My father doesn't (do not) work in Douala..."
(English *must* use the auxiliary "helper" verb "do" or "does" + "not" *before* the main verb.)

Why This Is Hard:
French has no "do/does" auxiliary. Students directly translate the "ne...pas" structure and say "My father *works not*..." or "My father *not work*...". They must learn to *always* add "do" or "does" for a simple present negative.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "My father *work not*..." or "...*works not*..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "My father *doesn't work*..."
Item ID: aux-5-A
Original Question: "___ you live near the school compound?" (Options: Do, Are, -)
Correct Answer: Do

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Tu habites près de l'école ?" (Statement with rising intonation)
"Est-ce que tu habites près de l'école ?" (Using "Est-ce que")

English Structure:
"Do you live near the school compound?"
(English *must* use the auxiliary "Do" or "Does" to start a simple "yes/no" question in the present.)

Why This Is Hard:
The most common way to ask a question in spoken French is to just raise the voice on a statement ("Tu habites...?"). Students translate this directly and say "You live...?", which is not a grammatically correct question in English.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "You live near the school...?"
- GREEN (Correct English): "Do you live near the school...?"
Item ID: aux-6-A
Original Question: "What time ___ the lesson finish?" (Options: is, do, does)
Correct Answer: does

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"À quelle heure est-ce que la leçon finit ?" or "...finit la leçon ?"
(French uses "est-ce que" or inverts the main verb. There is no auxiliary.)

English Structure:
"What time does the lesson finish?"
(English *must* use the auxiliary "do" or "does" *after* the question word. The pattern is: **QW + Auxiliary (does) + Subject (the lesson) + Verb (finish)**.)

Why This Is Hard:
As with "do", students forget to add "does". They translate the "est-ce que" structure and say "What time *the lesson finishes*?" or they try to invert the main verb, "What time *finishes the lesson*?".

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "What time *the lesson finishes*?"
- GREEN (Correct English): "What time *does the lesson finish*?"
Item ID: aux-3-A
Original Question: "I ___ Muna at the market yesterday." (Options: not see, didn't see, didn't saw)
Correct Answer: didn't see

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Je n'ai pas vu Muna au marché hier."
(French uses "ne...pas" around the auxiliary "ai" (have) from the *Passé Composé*.)

English Structure:
"I didn't (did not) see Muna at the market yesterday."
(English *must* use the past auxiliary "did" + "not", and it *must* be followed by the *base verb* ("see"), not the past tense form ("saw").)

Why This Is Hard:
This has two problems. First, students forget "did" (like they forget "do"). Second, after they use "didn't", they *also* put the main verb in the past ("didn't saw"), which is incorrect. They must learn: **didn't + see**.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "I *not saw*..." or "I *didn't saw*..."
- GREEN (Correct English): "I *didn't see*..."
Item ID: (Based on Common Error #7)
Original Question: "My parents ___ a car." (Options: have not, do not have, are not)
Correct Answer: do not have

CONTRASTIVE ANALYSIS:

French Structure:
"Mes parents n'ont pas de voiture."
(French negates the main verb "ont" (have) directly with "ne...pas".)

English Structure:
"My parents do not have a car."
(In English, when "have" is the *main verb* (meaning possession), it *also* needs the auxiliary "do/does" to become negative.)

Why This Is Hard:
This is a very specific and common error. Students translate "n'ont pas" directly to "have not". While "have not" *is* used when 'have' is an auxiliary ("I have not eaten"), it's *not* used when 'have' is the main verb.

Visual Highlighting:
- RED (French interference): "My parents *have not* a car."
- GREEN (Correct English): "My parents *do not have* a car."
